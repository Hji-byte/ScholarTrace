# Literature Review

What limitations and biases have been identified in LLM-as-a-judge evaluation, and which mitigation strategies have been proposed?

## Summary
The use of Large Language Models (LLMs) as automated judges has emerged as a scalable alternative to human annotation, but it is subject to various cognitive, social, and methodological biases [1, 3]. Key biases include position, verbosity, self-enhancement, and teacher preference biases [2, 3, 6], alongside broader social biases such as gender and authority bias [6, 8]. Methodological limitations encompass poor performance on math and reasoning tasks [3], evaluation frameworks that overemphasize helpfulness at the expense of safety [3], and a lack of theoretical interpretability in meta-evaluations [4]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed diverse mitigation strategies ranging from protocol-level adjustments [3, 5] to data-centric and model-centric debiasing techniques [2], including automated bias quantification frameworks [6, 7].

## Identified Biases in LLM Evaluators
LLM-as-a-judge models exhibit several specific cognitive and structural biases. They demonstrate position bias, showing a propensity to favor answers placed in certain positions over others [2, 3, 8]. Additionally, judges display verbosity bias, where increasing response length without a corresponding improvement in quality can alter model preference scores [2, 3, 6, 8]. Models also exhibit significant self-enhancement bias, rating their own outputs more favorably than others even when answer sources are anonymized [2, 3, 6, 8]. In the context of proxy judge models, training on evaluation data generated by powerful teacher models introduces teacher preference bias, causing the proxy to inherit biased preferences for the teacher model's responses [2]. 

Beyond these structural biases, LLM judges are susceptible to specific social and cognitive biases, including misinformation oversight, gender, authority, beauty, and compassion-fade biases [6, 8]. Inherent biases embedded in training data or prompt design further pose challenges to the fairness and reliability of LLM-based assessments [1]. In total, researchers have identified up to 12 key potential biases in LLM-as-a-judge systems, highlighting the pervasive nature of these issues [6].

## Methodological and Evaluation Limitations
The application of LLM-as-a-judge faces several methodological and evaluation limitations. Task-specific failures are prominent; LLM judges have limited capability in grading math and reasoning questions, often failing on basic problems they can solve independently because they are misled by the provided answers [3]. Evaluation frameworks such as MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena are limited by their overemphasis on helpfulness at the expense of safety metrics like honesty and harmlessness, and by conflating multiple helpfulness dimensions into a single metric [3]. 

Furthermore, current meta-evaluations lack theoretical interpretability for bias definitions and often fail to account for internal inconsistencies by assuming judges make deterministic decisions [4]. Human subjectivity in establishing ground truth, combined with the limited size and scope of test data, complicates the reproducibility of bias findings and increases the risk of random interference [6]. Current approaches to bias evaluation also face critical limitations, including substantial resource requirements for bias identification, difficulties in acquiring representative safety datasets, and the absence of universally accepted bias metrics [7]. Finally, fine-tuning an LLM judge on high-scoring but biased responses significantly degrades its performance, highlighting the inherent risk of training on biased data [5].

## Proposed Mitigation Strategies and Calibration Techniques
To address these limitations and biases, researchers have proposed a variety of mitigation strategies at the protocol, model, and framework levels. Protocol-level strategies include swapping the order of answers and only declaring a win if an answer is preferred in both orders, or using few-shot examples to improve consistency [3]. Researchers also recommend utilizing judge models with better robustness rate metrics or randomizing the order of the answers being evaluated to mitigate positional and verbosity biases [6]. Preemptive bias scanning using a secondary detection prompt can identify problematic patterns like emotional language or flattery before the judge model scores the response [5]. Other protocol-level recommendations include establishing objective evaluation criteria and using ensemble judging with human oversight for high-stakes decisions [5].

At the model and data level, an assistant-guided three-stage approach mitigates teacher preference bias by introducing an additional smaller assistant LLM into the training pipeline to filter out biased feedback and highlight key flaws [2]. Discussion-based debiasing methods mitigate single-LLM bias by leveraging multiple LLMs to engage in debate and deliberation, while adversarial debiasing methods address biases by introducing structured rationale pairs or constructing specially curated debiasing datasets [2]. Synthesized judging programs have also been proposed as a low-cost, transparent, and bias-mitigated alternative to traditional LLM-as-a-judge approaches [1]. 

Model calibration techniques, including probability- or prompt-level calibration, can be applied to down-weight superficial qualities during the scoring process [5]. The limited capability of LLMs in grading math and reasoning questions can be addressed using a reference-guided method [3]. To resolve score-comparison and pairwise transitivity inconsistencies, TrustJudge, a probabilistic evaluation framework, has been proposed [9]. Furthermore, the CALM framework systematically quantifies and analyzes biases by using automated and principle-guided modifications to the evaluation process, addressing the lack of universally accepted metrics and reducing reliance on manual annotation [6, 7].

## Limitations and Open Problems
Despite the proposed mitigation strategies, several limitations and open problems remain. The performance of probabilistic frameworks like TrustJudge is fundamentally dependent on the instruction-following capabilities of the employed evaluation models, which are often weaker in smaller-scale language models [9]. The risk of training on biased data remains a critical vulnerability, as fine-tuning on high-scoring yet biased responses significantly degrades judge performance [5]. 

Current bias evaluation approaches still struggle with substantial resource requirements, difficulties in acquiring representative safety datasets, and the absence of universally accepted bias metrics [7]. Additionally, human subjectivity in establishing ground truth and the limited scope of test data continue to complicate the reproducibility of bias findings and increase the risk of random interference [6]. Finally, evaluation frameworks must evolve beyond single aggregated metrics, as current benchmarks often conflate multiple dimensions of helpfulness while neglecting crucial safety metrics like honesty and harmlessness [3].
