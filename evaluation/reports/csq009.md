# Literature Review

What causes over-smoothing and over-squashing in graph neural networks, and which mitigation methods are supported by empirical and theoretical research?

## Summary
Graph neural networks (GNNs) suffer from two primary pathologies as depth increases: over-smoothing and over-squashing [2, 5, 7]. Over-smoothing is driven by vanishing gradients and positive graph curvature, causing node representations to become indistinguishable [1, 6]. Over-squashing arises from graph bottlenecks and negative curvature, preventing the effective aggregation of long-range information [3, 6]. Mitigation strategies range from architectural modifications like residual connections and state-space models to topology-aware graph rewiring based on discrete curvature [1, 3, 7]. However, many existing methods either lack deep theoretical guarantees or substantially alter the original graph topology, highlighting the need for scalable, topology-preserving approaches [2, 4].

## Causes of Over-Smoothing and Over-Squashing
Over-smoothing occurs when node representations become overly homogeneous or nearly indistinguishable with increasing depth, which restricts expressive power and creates numerical difficulties during training [2, 5, 7]. Theoretically, vanishing gradients can cause a specific form of over-smoothing where all signals converge exactly to a unique fixed point [1]. Furthermore, the over-smoothing phenomenon observed in practice is often a mixture of pure over-smoothing and the difficulty of optimizing weights in deep GNN models [8]. From a geometric perspective, over-smoothing is linked to positive Ollivier-Ricci graph curvature, as positively curved edges cause node representations to become similar [6]. Similarly, edges contributing to over-smoothing exhibit high Forman-Ricci curvature [3]. Augmentations of Forman-Ricci curvature can be used to study these effects, with proven upper and lower bounds that depend on node degrees [3].

Over-squashing refers to the inability of GNNs to accurately leverage information encoded in long-range connections [3]. This happens when the number of nodes within a receptive field grows exponentially with the number of layers, squashing exponentially growing amounts of information into fixed-size node features [6]. Over-squashing is caused by bottlenecks in the graph structure and is theoretically linked to negative Ollivier-Ricci graph curvature, as low curvature values characterize these bottlenecks [3, 6]. Edges underlying long-range connections that contribute to over-squashing exhibit low graph curvature [3].

## Mitigation Techniques for Over-Smoothing
Standard mitigation techniques for over-smoothing include normalization layers, residual connections, and random edge dropout, though these methods often lack deep theoretical understanding and have limited ability to prevent over-smoothing in very deep models [2, 5]. A unified theoretical perspective based on signed graphs reveals that these techniques implicitly introduce negative edges to alter message-passing and resist over-smoothing [5]. Building on this, the Label-SBP method mitigates over-smoothing by reducing the Structural Imbalance Degree to create a more structurally balanced graph, providing theoretical guarantees on the achieved level of structure balance [5]. Additionally, adding residual connections has been provably shown to mitigate over-smoothing, with explicit convergence rates for a normalized vertex similarity measure grounded in the multiplicative ergodic theorem [7].

Other architectural and algorithmic modifications include dilated convolutions, dense connections, and iteratively learning graph structures [7, 9]. Graph convolutional filters inspired by graph signal processing have also been developed to avoid over-smoothing [9]. Subgraph-based approaches, such as training GNNs of arbitrary depths using localized subgraphs, offer another mitigation strategy [9]. Furthermore, propagation-based methods like Personalized PageRank (PPR) and its approximate variant (APPNP) mitigate over-smoothing while retaining the ability to propagate neural predictions [8].

## Mitigation Techniques for Over-Squashing
Mitigating over-squashing is theoretically and empirically best achieved through a combination of strong graph connectivity and non-dissipative dynamics, such as controlling the layerwise Jacobian via state-space-inspired models [1]. To address the geometric causes of over-squashing, graph rewiring techniques based on Augmented Forman-Ricci curvature can mitigate both over-smoothing and over-squashing simultaneously by removing high-curvature edges and adding edges to low-curvature neighborhoods in linear time [3]. Similarly, the Batch Ollivier-Ricci Flow algorithm leverages the link between over-squashing and negative graph curvature to simultaneously address both issues [6]. There is a formal trade-off between over-smoothing and over-squashing, meaning that mitigating both simultaneously requires both edge addition and edge removal rather than relying solely on one [9]. The SJLR method addresses these fundamental problems, outperforming previous methods in both homophilous and heterophilous graph datasets [9].

Alternative approaches avoid altering the original graph topology by using expanded width-aware message passing or learned cooperative mechanisms to alleviate information bottlenecks [4]. Additionally, over-squashing can be mitigated by generalizing Hyperbolic GNNs to Riemannian manifolds of variable curvature, making the geometry of the embedding space faithful to the graph's topology [10].

## Limitations and Open Problems
Many existing methods to mitigate over-squashing, such as graph rewiring, spectrum-preserving sparsification, and constructing new graphs from node features, substantially alter or completely discard the original graph topology [2, 4]. While standard mitigation techniques for over-smoothing (e.g., normalization, residual connections, edge dropout) are empirically effective, their ability to prevent over-smoothing is often limited in very deep models [2, 5]. Furthermore, existing curvature-based rewiring methods often require expensive subroutines and careful hyperparameter tuning, which limits their applicability to large-scale graphs [3]. Future research could explore integrating neural architecture search into curvature-based methods to facilitate hyperparameter optimization [9].
