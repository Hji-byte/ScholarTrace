# Literature Review

How have text-to-SQL systems evolved from schema-aware neural parsers to large-language-model-based approaches, and which challenges remain on cross-domain benchmarks?

## Summary
The evolution of text-to-SQL systems has transitioned through rule-based, neural network-based, pre-trained language model (PLM)-based, and large language model (LLM)-based stages [3, 4]. While early neural parsers and PLMs laid the groundwork for schema-aware parsing [1, 2], modern LLM-based approaches leverage vast linguistic knowledge and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to handle complex queries and cross-domain generalization [2]. Despite these architectural advancements, persistent challenges remain on cross-domain benchmarks, including difficulties in adapting to unseen schemas [2, 4, 5], inherent benchmark ambiguities [6], high computational costs [2, 3], and a lack of dynamic schema adaptation [2].

## Evolution of Text-to-SQL Architectures
The evolution of text-to-SQL systems has progressed through four distinct stages: rule-based, neural network-based, pre-trained language model (PLM)-based, and large language model (LLM)-based [3, 4]. Early rule-based systems relied on manually crafted grammar rules and heuristics, which were limited in handling complex queries and single-table scenarios [2, 3]. Deep learning approaches subsequently introduced sequence-to-sequence architectures, LSTMs, and transformers, enabling end-to-end differentiable systems like Seq-2-SQL and SQLNet [2]. The neural network-based stage further advanced capabilities from single-table to multi-table scenarios by incorporating graph neural networks and relation-aware transformers, such as RAT-SQL and RASAT, to manage complex queries and enhance synonym handling [1, 3]. Models like PICARD also improved performance on multi-turn SQL generation tasks [1]. However, these neural methods were limited by model size and training data availability, and they often struggled with structural awareness and rare or complex SQL operations, such as nested subqueries and window functions [3, 4].

Around 2018, the field transitioned to PLMs, fine-tuning models like BERT, RoBERTa, and TaBERT on standard datasets to leverage vast linguistic knowledge [2, 3, 4]. Models like X-SQL integrated contextual outputs from BERT-style models to improve schema representation [1]. While PLMs improved generalization to unseen databases by capturing dependencies between natural language queries and schemas, they still required domain-specific fine-tuning and struggled with complex schemas without additional schema linking [2]. Around 2020, LLMs emerged, bringing significant capabilities in natural language understanding as model scale increased [3, 4]. LLMs address linguistic complexity and ambiguity more effectively than traditional and earlier neural models by leveraging vast training datasets and techniques like chain-of-thought prompting [2, 4]. The architecture of modern LLM-based text-to-SQL systems typically comprises four key phases: natural language understanding, schema comprehension, SQL generation, and SQL execution [2].

## Large Language Models and Retrieval-Augmented Generation
To address persistent limitations in schema understanding, linguistic ambiguity, complex query handling, and domain generalization, modern LLM-based systems integrate Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [2]. Graph RAG, in particular, improves contextual accuracy and schema linking by modeling database schemas through graph-based relationships rather than flat schema descriptions [2]. These structured graph connections are critical for tackling the complexity of modern databases in cross-domain scenarios by capturing relationships between tables, columns, and entities [2].

## Persistent Challenges on Cross-Domain Benchmarks
Cross-domain benchmark datasets such as Spider, Spider 2.0, WikiSQL, and KaggleDBA are designed to evaluate the generalization capabilities of models across unseen databases and query structures [1, 2]. Spider is a large-scale, human-labeled dataset, while Spider 2.0 features 632 real-world workflow problems sourced from enterprise databases [1]. Despite these benchmarks, text-to-SQL models struggle with cross-domain generalization because they fail to adapt to variations in vocabulary, schema structures, and query patterns [2, 4, 5]. Furthermore, major text-to-SQL benchmarks suffer from pervasive challenges related to wrong assumptions on database content and ambiguities in schema matching, making it impossible to achieve perfect accuracy even with a perfect model [6].

Beyond benchmark limitations, current text-to-SQL systems face several operational challenges. LLM-based methods incur high token consumption during inference, resulting in high computational costs and low efficiency when processing queries for large and complex databases [2, 3]. Most systems lack effective incremental learning and flexible architectures, making them inefficient at adapting to dynamic, evolving database schemas without requiring full retraining [2]. Additionally, existing methods are typically restricted to single, fixed databases, limiting their capability to handle open-domain scenarios that require cross-database queries and multi-source data aggregation [3]. The majority of methods also lack interpretability and debugging mechanisms, hindering users from understanding the SQL generation process and detecting semantic errors [3]. Finally, current systems rely heavily on high-quality training data and show limited adaptability to new domains [3].

## Limitations and Open Problems
Critical remaining challenges for text-to-SQL systems include extending support to NoSQL databases, improving query efficiency, and handling domain-specific complexities [1]. Additional open problems involve enhancing computational efficiency, model robustness, data privacy, and supporting multi-turn conversational interactions [1, 2]. Addressing the automatic generation of targeted training samples based on model feedback remains an open problem to improve adaptability to new domains without relying exclusively on high-quality static training data [3].
