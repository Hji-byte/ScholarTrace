# Literature Review

What limitations of adversarial training as a defense for image classifiers have been established, particularly regarding accuracy, computational cost, and generalization to unseen attacks?

## Summary
Adversarial training is widely recognized as a primary defense mechanism for image classifiers against adversarial examples [1, 6, 7]. However, established literature highlights significant limitations across multiple dimensions. Defenders face a fundamental trade-off between clean accuracy on natural images and robustness to adversarial perturbations [3, 6], alongside severe computational overheads driven by the intractability of exact worst-case loss minimization [5, 7]. Furthermore, adversarial training exhibits poor generalization to unseen, adaptive, and physically realizable attacks [2, 5, 7], and suffers from training instabilities such as overfitting and oscillations that degrade long-term defense efficacy [6, 8].

## Accuracy Trade-offs and Human Alignment
Adversarial training improves a classifier's robustness against adversarial examples but causes a significant drop in clean accuracy on natural, unaltered images, demonstrating a clear trade-off between standard accuracy and adversarial robustness [1, 6, 7]. This gap is substantial; despite state-of-the-art clean accuracy reaching about 100% on datasets like CIFAR-10, state-of-the-art adversarial training robustness to $\ell_\infty$-norm bounded perturbations barely exceeds 70% [3]. This clean accuracy drop can be partially mitigated by training on a 50/50 mix of clean and adversarial samples, though this mitigation comes at the cost of reduced adversarial robustness [6]. Alternatively, localized adversarial training can mitigate this loss by forcing the model to focus on image foregrounds instead of backgrounds [1]. Although adversarial training imposes a slight clean accuracy penalty on generic image classification datasets, it does not inherently increase training-time computational intensity compared to more complex defensive structures [4]. 

Beyond the numerical trade-off, the standard $\ell_\infty$ attack formulation used in adversarial training is flawed because it creates invalid adversarial data that humans also misclassify, indicating a misalignment between the training objective and human perception [3].

## Computational Overhead and Resource Requirements
The robust loss minimization objective underlying adversarial training is a highly intractable problem that requires approximate solutions [7]. Generating adversarial examples for training incurs a computational overhead proportional to the number of attack iterations. For example, using 10 PGD steps and the TRADES loss requires approximately 9 times the FLOPs of a regular training iteration, making the overall loop 27 times the FLOPs of a single forward pass [3, 10]. This overhead becomes prohibitive for resource-constrained devices [10]. 

The computational costs are further exacerbated by the necessity of using larger models and larger datasets to improve adversarial robustness [3]. Additionally, state-of-the-art adversarial training methods diverge from compute-optimal setups, utilizing excess compute for their achieved level of robustness [3]. To address these costs, augmentation strategies like DAJAT can improve the robustness-accuracy trade-off and achieve robust accuracy at half the computational cost of baseline adversarial training methods [9].

## Generalization to Unseen and Physical Attacks
Adversarial training often fails to generalize to new or unseen attacks because computing the exact worst-case loss is computationally intractable, forcing defenders to minimize the loss with respect to specific heuristic attack strategies [5]. Consequently, models tend to exhibit robustness only to the specific attacks they were trained on and are easily broken by stronger, previously unseen attacks [2, 5]. This limitation extends to the physical domain, where adversarial training with PGD attacks exhibits limited effectiveness when defending against high-profile physically realizable attacks [7]. Highlighting these generalization limitations, generative classifiers constructed from pre-trained diffusion models have been shown to defend against multiple unseen threats without requiring training on particular adversarial attacks [2].

## Training Instabilities and Overfitting
Beyond generalization and cost, adversarial training is prone to overfitting on the training set, which degrades its defense efficacy and generalization to unseen data [6, 8]. This overfitting results in a loss of robust accuracy on the test set during the later epochs of training [6, 8]. Furthermore, existing adversarial training methods exhibit apparent oscillations during the training process, which further degrades their overall defense efficacy [8].

## Limitations and Open Problems
The available evidence establishes that adversarial training remains constrained by a fundamental trade-off between clean and robust accuracy, which is further complicated by human misalignment in standard threat models. Computationally, the intractability of exact worst-case loss minimization forces reliance on expensive heuristic approximations, rendering the process prohibitive for resource-constrained environments and leading to compute-inefficient scaling. Generalization remains a critical failure mode, as defenses trained on specific heuristics fail against unseen, adaptive, and physically realizable attacks. Finally, training instabilities such as overfitting and oscillations continue to degrade long-term defense efficacy. Open problems include developing compute-optimal training methods, formulating human-aligned threat models, and achieving robust generalization across diverse and physical threat landscapes without sacrificing clean accuracy.
