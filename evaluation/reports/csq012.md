# Literature Review

How do diffusion-based and autoregressive image generation models compare in training objectives, sampling cost, controllability, and output quality?

## Summary
Diffusion and autoregressive models represent two primary paradigms for image generation, each with distinct training objectives, sampling characteristics, controllability mechanisms, and output qualities. While diffusion models offer advanced, widely adopted control mechanisms [4], they lag behind autoregressive models in likelihood modeling [3] and suffer from high computational costs due to iterative denoising [5]. Conversely, autoregressive models leverage mature large language model infrastructures to offer lower computational costs and high scalability [4, 5]. However, autoregressive models have historically lagged in controllability [4] and face efficiency bottlenecks when integrating diffusion sampling [1] or relying on sequential token prediction [4].

## Training Objectives and Loss Functions
The training objectives of diffusion and autoregressive models differ fundamentally in their loss functions and data requirements. Autoregressive models operating purely in a next-token-prediction paradigm can be trained using simple cross-entropy loss with discrete inputs and outputs [5]. For instance, the D-AR framework models image generation using standard autoregressive next-token prediction, avoiding the need to customize attention masks or vary training loss functions [5]. Alternatively, autoregressive models can be trained using a combination of standard cross-entropy loss for discrete text tokens and flow matching loss for continuous image tokens, where the flow matching loss is the mean squared error between predicted and target velocity vectors mapping a noised patch to a clean patch [6]. However, a primary bottleneck for autoregressive models that quantize images into discrete tokens is the introduction of quantization errors [1]. 

In terms of data efficiency, supervised fine-tuning in autoregressive frameworks requires datasets at the million-sample scale to yield substantial improvements, whereas diffusion models can maintain stable and general image generation with only a few thousand samples [6]. Despite these differences in training, diffusion models lag behind autoregressive models in likelihood modeling [3].

## Sampling Efficiency and Computational Costs
Sampling efficiency and computational costs present a complex trade-off between the two paradigms. Diffusion models generate high-quality visual content by iteratively denoising from initial noise, but this dense process requires significant sampling steps and is computationally expensive [5]. In contrast, autoregressive models offer lower computational costs by formulating image generation as a sequence prediction problem that aligns with large language model representations [4]. This alignment allows autoregressive methods to leverage established optimization techniques from large language models, such as KV-caching, to increase sampling speed [7]. Furthermore, diffusion models offer the potential for parallelized generation, providing an inference efficiency advantage over sequential autoregressive models [3].

However, the reliance on sequential token prediction in autoregressive visual generation models can sometimes limit overall efficiency [4]. This bottleneck is exacerbated when autoregressive models adopt diffusion sampling to generate continuous tokens, which typically requires 50 to 100 diffusion steps to sample a single token, leading to low inference efficiency [1]. To mitigate this, applying diffusion step scheduling to autoregressive models can accelerate inference by 5 to 10 times for models like MAR and Harmon, and 1.4 to 2.5 times for FlowAR and xAR, without sacrificing generation quality [1]. Additionally, incorporating control conditions into autoregressive models via conditional prefilling increases training memory by 59.1% and training latency by 96.3% compared to conditional decoding [2].

## Controllability and Conditioning Mechanisms
Diffusion models have historically dominated controllable image generation, producing numerous widely adopted methods such as ControlNet and T2I-Adapter that offer advanced control mechanisms [4]. Consequently, autoregressive models have remained under-explored in terms of controllability and flexibility, and early autoregressive approaches for controllable generation suffered from efficiency issues like overly long sequences [4]. Control-to-image generation has been largely unexplored in autoregressive models compared to diffusion models [2].

Recent advancements, however, demonstrate that autoregressive models can achieve precise control without compromising image quality [2]. Controllable autoregressive frameworks like CAR can serve as more efficient and scalable controllable generative paradigms than diffusion-based models like ControlNet [4]. Furthermore, autoregressive models can be extended to generate arbitrary-resolution images through conditional decoding and multi-resolution training, which enhances their control capabilities [2].

## Output Quality and Evaluation Metrics
Both diffusion and autoregressive models are capable of generating high-quality visual content, with recent autoregressive models achieving generative capabilities comparable to diffusion models [4, 5]. Autoregressive models demonstrate impressive generative quality and scalability for visual generation by leveraging mature training and inference infrastructures from large language models [4]. For example, a 775M-parameter LLaMA backbone operating in a next-token-prediction paradigm achieved a 2.09 gFID on the ImageNet class-conditioned generation benchmark, proving that autoregressive models are strong competitors to diffusion models in output quality [5].

Incorporating diffusion sampling into autoregressive models to generate continuous tokens significantly improves image generation quality [1]. In the realm of controllable generation, autoregressive models designed for this purpose are highly competitive with diffusion model-based state-of-the-art methods in maintaining image quality under a variety of control conditions [2]. Notably, the ControlAR framework enables autoregressive models to surpass state-of-the-art controllable diffusion models, such as ControlNet++, in both quantitative and qualitative controllability and image quality [2].

## Limitations and Open Problems
Despite significant progress, both paradigms face distinct limitations. Diffusion models are hindered by their computationally expensive, dense iterative denoising processes that require significant sampling steps [5]. Autoregressive models, while benefiting from LLM optimizations, are constrained by their reliance on sequential token prediction, which can limit overall efficiency [4]. When autoregressive models integrate diffusion sampling to improve quality, they suffer from severe inference inefficiencies, requiring dozens of steps per token [1]. Furthermore, autoregressive models that rely on discrete tokenization struggle with quantization errors [1]. Finally, autoregressive models exhibit poor data efficiency during supervised fine-tuning, requiring million-scale datasets to achieve substantial improvements, unlike diffusion models which can generalize well with only a few thousand samples [6]. Future research must address these computational and data-efficiency bottlenecks to fully unify the strengths of both paradigms.
