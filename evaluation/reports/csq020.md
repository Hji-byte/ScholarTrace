# Literature Review

How do black-box and white-box membership inference attacks against machine learning models compare in their assumptions, methods, and evaluations?

## Summary
This literature review compares black-box and white-box membership inference attacks against machine learning models, examining their underlying assumptions, methodologies, and evaluations [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7]. While black-box attacks rely on model outputs and are extensively studied, white-box attacks leverage internal parameters and gradients to exploit training algorithm vulnerabilities [2, 3, 4, 7]. Theoretical work suggests black-box access can be optimal under specific assumptions, but empirical evidence demonstrates that white-box attacks can significantly outperform black-box methods in practice, particularly for well-generalized models [2, 4, 5, 7]. However, white-box methods face challenges such as feature misalignment and high computational costs for rigorous evaluation [2, 3, 4].

## Threat Models and Adversary Knowledge
White-box membership inference attacks assume the attacker has full access to the model's internal parameters, architecture, and computations, such as loss values, logits, activations, and gradient norms [1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. In contrast, black-box attacks assume access is restricted to model outputs like predicted labels or confidence scores [1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Black-box threat models typically assume the adversary has access to the learning algorithm and a finite dataset sampled from the training distribution [1, 4]. However, some white-box attacks operate under relaxed threat models where the attacker is granted access to a portion of the target model's training data, such as up to half, to improve attack efficacy [4]. Conversely, the strongest recent black-box methods rely on reference models and assumptions about the target model's training configuration and candidate set that are unlikely to hold in practice [3].

## Methodologies and Attack Vectors
Black-box attacks primarily exploit the statistical differences in a model's confidence or predictions between its training set and unseen data [2, 7]. A straightforward extension of black-box attacks to the white-box setting—such as analyzing the outputs of activation functions—is often ineffective because early layers generalize too quickly, final layers leak no more information than the output layer, and these functions can generalize in well-regularized models [2]. Instead, white-box attacks are designed by exploiting the privacy vulnerabilities of the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm [2, 3, 4]. For instance, attackers can leverage per-example gradients and gradient norms, since SGD approximately brings these gradients to zero on training points at convergence [2, 3, 4]. Other white-box methods include the Inverse Hessian Attack (IHA), which explicitly uses model parameters by computing inverse-Hessian vector products [5], and optimization-based attacks like ImpMIA, which use Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions to identify training samples whose gradients most strongly reconstruct the model's parameters, thereby eliminating the need for shadow models [3]. However, white-box attacks that attempt to learn internal feature properties from shadow models often fail because internal features are unlikely to reside at the exact same network locations in the target model, forcing these methods to require large amounts of the target model's actual training data [4].

## Theoretical and Empirical Comparisons
Theoretical and empirical comparisons between black-box and white-box attacks reveal a complex landscape. Under specific theoretical assumptions, the Bayes-optimal membership inference strategy depends only on the loss function, indicating that black-box attacks can theoretically perform as well as white-box attacks [1, 3, 5]. However, these theoretical assumptions do not hold for most practical models trained with SGD, indicating that optimal membership inference actually requires white-box access [5]. This theoretical limitation aligns with empirical work showing that white-box attacks can outperform black-box attacks by double-digit accuracy gaps [7]. White-box adversaries can design attacks at least as strong as black-box attacks by combining output layer signals with additional signals from internal layers, such as activations or gradients [7]. Furthermore, white-box attacks can exploit a model's idiosyncratic use of features and internal memorization to infer membership even when the model's black-box behavior appears to generalize well [4]. Specific white-box attacks leveraging implicit bias or calibrated model memorization can achieve state-of-the-art performance and outperform prior black-box methods when the attacker knows the model weights and has a superset of the training data [3, 4]. 

Regarding evaluation, evaluating the true positive rate at very low false positive rates provides a stricter and more meaningful measure of membership inference risk for real-world deployments [3]. State-of-the-art black-box membership inference attacks often require significant computational resources to evaluate, such as training 256 reference models per experiment for baselines [3]. Black-box membership inference attacks are most likely to succeed when the model is overfitted, the targeted records are outliers or subpopulations, or the auxiliary data is abundant and from the same distribution [7]. In contrast, well-generalized machine learning models are significantly susceptible to white-box membership inference attacks that exploit the privacy vulnerabilities of the SGD algorithm [2]. Additionally, a simple black-box method based on N-Gram coverage has been shown to perform on par with or better than white-box methods that require model loss or token logits, while being substantially more compute-efficient [9]. Nevertheless, in standard evaluation scenarios, basic black-box attack methods often remain the strongest approaches relative to existing white-box approaches [3].

## Limitations and Open Problems
While black-box membership inference attacks have been extensively studied, comparatively few works have developed white-box membership inference attacks [7]. The theoretical assumptions used to prove that black-box access is sufficient for optimal membership inference do not hold for most practical models trained with stochastic gradient descent, indicating a need for further exploration of white-box methods [5]. Furthermore, extending black-box attacks to the white-box setting by simply analyzing activation functions is ineffective, and methods that rely on shadow models to learn internal feature properties often fail due to feature misalignment across networks [2, 4]. State-of-the-art black-box methods also rely on assumptions about the target model's training configuration and candidate sets that are unlikely to hold in practice, highlighting a gap between theoretical evaluations and real-world applicability [3]. Finally, evaluating attacks at very low false positive rates remains computationally expensive, as it often requires training hundreds of reference models [3].
