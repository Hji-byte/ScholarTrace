# Literature Review

Which automatic and human evaluation methods are used to measure hallucination and factual consistency in large language models, and what evidence exists about their reliability?

## Summary
Evaluating hallucination and factual consistency in large language models relies on a combination of automatic metrics and human evaluation protocols. While human evaluation remains the most reliable approach, it is costly and difficult to scale [7]. Automatic metrics, including traditional overlap measures, specialized non-LLM models, and LLM-as-a-judge approaches, show varying degrees of correlation with human judgments [1, 4]. Specialized non-LLM metrics generally outperform LLM-based evaluators in capturing diverse hallucination error types, though LLM-as-a-judge methods can achieve high agreement under specific conditions [2, 4].

## Automatic Metrics and Benchmarks for Hallucination Evaluation
Traditional automatic text summarization metrics such as ROUGE, BLEU, and BERTScore exhibit poor correlations with human judgments regarding factual consistency [4]. Specifically, ROUGE-L correlates well with human ratings for short-answer English tasks but is unreliable in free-form generation tasks and cross-lingual scenarios [1]. Early factual consistency evaluation methods included unsupervised and weakly-supervised metrics such as relational triple-based, textual-entailment-based, and question-answering (QA)-based techniques [3]. However, traditional QA-based approaches are considered complicated and inefficient because they require separate models to perform question generation, answer selection, and answering [3]. Furthermore, these entailment- and QA-based metrics struggle with plain language summarization because elaborative explanations introduce external content absent from the source document [7]. Overall, traditional automatic factuality metrics based on answering or syntactic dependency fail to achieve a correlation score above 0.3 with human evaluations [3].

To address these limitations, state-of-the-art non-LLM-based factuality metrics such as SummaC-ZS, SummaC-CV, QAFactEval, and AlignScore have been developed [4]. While these automated metrics effectively verify source-supported content, they fail to assess the factual consistency of added information and rely heavily on high-quality reference summaries [7]. 

Several benchmarks have been introduced to evaluate these methods. TruthfulQA, UHGEval, and HalluDial focus on assessing the truthfulness and reliability of LLM-generated content to quantify hallucinations [2]. The Factual Inconsistency Benchmark (FIB) evaluates factual consistency in summarization by measuring the proportion of times a model assigns a higher score to a factually consistent summary than to a factually inconsistent one [6]. Additionally, the TofuEval benchmark evaluates hallucinations in dialogue summarization using professional linguistic annotators [4].

## Human Evaluation Protocols and Tradeoffs
Human evaluation for factual consistency is considered reliable but is costly, difficult to scale, and often requires specialized domain expertise [7]. Despite its high labor cost and low efficiency, human review is utilized in benchmarks like HIC-Bench to ensure the reliability of automated evaluations of hallucinations [2]. In the HIC-Bench framework, automated evaluation is followed by human reviewers performing token-level analysis on the model's evaluation outputs to enhance reliability [2]. 

Professional linguistic data annotators are also employed in benchmarks such as TofuEval to perform binary factuality evaluation of summary sentences and provide explanations for hallucinated contents [4]. When crowdsourcing is utilized, human evaluation of fact inconsistency can be conducted via platforms like Amazon Mechanical Turk using highly qualified annotators at a cost of approximately $0.30 per sentence [8].

## Correlation and Reliability of Evaluation Methods
The correlation between automatic and human evaluation methods varies significantly based on the metric and context. Standard correlation metrics are limited in dealing with ties and constant scores common in human annotations, leading researchers to use Pairwise Accuracy as an alternative [1]. Furthermore, evaluating factuality metrics requires controlling for dataset biases and confounding variables associated with system and dataset properties, which can be addressed by utilizing partial correlation coefficients [3].

LLM-as-a-judge approaches show mixed reliability. While LLMs-as-a-judge can align closely with human expert judgments in certain contexts [2], and GPT-4 aligns with human evaluations at over 80% agreement—reaching levels comparable to human-to-human agreement—it is the only LLM-based evaluator found to not be inferior to non-LLM factuality metrics in certain benchmarks [1, 4]. However, the effectiveness of using GPT-4 as an automatic judge diminishes significantly if reference answers are not included in the prompt [1]. The reliability of LLM-as-a-judge is also influenced by evaluation design, decoding strategies, and Chain-of-Thought reasoning [5]. More broadly, most LLM-based evaluation approaches fail to show a significant correlation with human evaluations across most factual error types, indicating a fundamental limitation in current LLMs' ability to assess factuality [3]. 

When used as binary factual consistency evaluators, large language models, including GPT-4, perform poorly at detecting factual errors in LLM-generated dialogue summaries and are outperformed by specialized non-LLM-based factuality metrics [4]. Non-LLM-based metrics are better at capturing diverse hallucination error types and are generally found to be superior to LLM-based evaluators for detecting hallucinations in model-generated summaries [2, 4]. Regarding model scaling, larger LLMs do not necessarily generate more factually consistent dialogue summaries than smaller models [4], although LLMs generally become more factually consistent as their parameter sizes scale up, even though they can still be fooled by artificially generated factually inconsistent summaries at rates similar to model-generated inconsistencies [6].

## Limitations and Open Problems
The evaluation of hallucination and factual consistency in large language models faces several persistent challenges. Automatic metrics struggle to evaluate added information and are highly dependent on the availability of high-quality reference summaries [7]. LLM-as-a-judge methods require careful prompt design, including the provision of reference answers, and still fail to capture diverse hallucination error types as effectively as specialized non-LLM metrics [1, 4]. Human evaluation remains the gold standard for reliability but is hindered by high costs, scalability issues, and the need for domain expertise [2, 7]. Additionally, methodological limitations, such as the inability of standard correlation metrics to handle ties [1] and the presence of dataset biases [3], complicate the accurate assessment of evaluation metric reliability. Future work must address these gaps by developing more robust automatic metrics capable of handling open-ended generation and by establishing standardized, bias-controlled evaluation protocols.
