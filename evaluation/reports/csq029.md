# Literature Review

Which benchmarks and metrics are used to evaluate sparse, dense, and hybrid neural information retrieval, and how well do they measure out-of-domain robustness?

## Summary
This review examines the benchmarks, metrics, and methodologies used to evaluate sparse, dense, and hybrid neural information retrieval (IR) models, with a specific focus on out-of-domain (OOD) robustness. While standard in-domain benchmarks like MSMARCO and TREC DL are common, specialized benchmarks such as BEIR [3], BestIR [1], and Mr. TyDi [7] are utilized to assess OOD generalization and adversarial robustness. Standard ranking metrics (e.g., NDCG@K, Recall@K) and specialized drop-rate metrics are employed to quantify robustness [1, 4]. Evidence indicates that sparse retrievers generally exhibit greater OOD robustness than dense retrievers [2], while hybrid models combine the strengths of both to improve generalization [5], though all models remain vulnerable to adversarial attacks [5]. Current evaluation frameworks face limitations in capturing the full spectrum of real-world OOD scenarios [1].

## Benchmarks and Datasets for Neural Retrieval
Evaluating neural IR models requires a mix of in-domain and out-of-domain benchmarks. MSMARCO and TREC DL are standardly utilized as in-domain benchmarks, whereas BEIR serves as the primary out-of-domain benchmark for evaluating retrieval model robustness [2]. The BEIR benchmark comprises 18 datasets across 9 diverse retrieval tasks and text domains, specifically designed to evaluate the out-of-distribution generalization capabilities of sparse, dense, lexical, late-interaction, and re-ranking models in a zero-shot setting [3]. Evaluations on BEIR have revealed that while BM25 is a robust baseline, dense and sparse neural models often underperform in generalization [3]. 

To comprehensively evaluate robustness, the BestIR benchmark was introduced to study generalizability to unseen documents and unseen queries across seven types of adversarial and out-of-distribution scenarios [1]. For multilingual evaluation, the Mr. TyDi benchmark assesses mono-lingual retrieval across eleven languages using BM25, mDPR, and sparse-dense hybrids, revealing that dense retrieval techniques struggle to generalize to out-of-distribution inputs [7]. Other specialized benchmarks include KILT, which evaluates generative IR models primarily using R-precision [4], and HaluBench, which evaluates sparse, dense, and hybrid pipelines for hallucination mitigation using order-aware metrics [6]. Datasets for adversarial robustness are typically constructed using basic original datasets, expanded datasets to model unknown queries or documents, or off-the-shelf datasets customized for attack and defense evaluation [1].

## Evaluation Metrics and Robustness Definitions
Robustness in information retrieval is formally categorized into in-distribution (IID) robustness, adversarial robustness, and out-of-distribution (OOD) robustness [1]. IID robustness is defined as the variance of a model's ranking performance on a test dataset being less than or equal to an acceptable error threshold [1]. OOD robustness is formally defined as a model's ability to keep the difference in a ranking metric between the original test distribution and a new test distribution within an acceptable error threshold [1]. Adversarial robustness refers to the model's ability to defend against adversarial attacks [1].

The ranking performance and OOD generalizability of neural IR models on unseen queries are typically evaluated using standard top-K metrics such as Recall@K, NDCG@K, MRR@K, and MAP, calculated as the average evaluation score across a test dataset [1]. To specifically measure OOD generalizability, the drop rate between OOD and IID retrieval performance, denoted as DROOD, is used to evaluate the performance degradation of retrieval models [4]. Adversarial robustness is evaluated by measuring retrieval performance (Recall@K) and the average performance drop compared to the original in-distribution dataset under adversarial attack sets [5]. During the training phase, metrics like CleanMRR@k are used to evaluate the ability of empirical and certified robustness methods to maintain the original ranked list in the presence of adversarial samples [1].

## Out-of-Domain Robustness of Sparse, Dense, and Hybrid Models
Empirical evaluations demonstrate distinct robustness profiles for sparse, dense, and hybrid retrieval architectures. Sparse retrieval models consistently outperform dense retrieval models on out-of-domain benchmarks, demonstrating greater robustness to distribution shifts; this performance advantage is more pronounced on out-of-domain evaluations than on in-domain evaluations [2]. Furthermore, dense retrieval models trained with knowledge distillation suffer from overfitting and experience a decline in out-of-domain performance at larger model scales, whereas sparse knowledge distillation models improve steadily [2]. 

Hybrid retrieval models leverage the strengths of both architectures to improve robustness. Light hybrid retrieval models improve upon both sparse and dense retrievers in out-of-distribution settings, achieving performance similar to established hybrid models like hybrid-DPR and SPAR [5]. Hybrid retrievers achieve better out-of-domain generalization and adversarial robustness than individual sparse or dense components, as dense retrieval provides valuable relevance signals when integrated into a sparse-dense hybrid model [5, 7]. However, despite these improvements, all evaluated retrievers experience performance degradation across adversarial attack sets compared to their original in-distribution performance, indicating that current retrievers still lack sufficient adversarial robustness [5].

## Limitations and Open Problems
Despite the development of diverse benchmarks, a major limitation in enhancing out-of-distribution robustness in information retrieval is the lack of harmonized benchmarks that accurately reflect a multitude of real-world OOD scenarios [1]. Without standardized benchmarks, it remains difficult to assess the true robustness of IR systems across different contexts and to identify specific areas for improvement [1]. Additionally, current evaluations of generative IR models highlight a need to introduce new web search datasets to simulate broader out-of-distribution environments, including adversarial attacks [4]. Future research must focus on creating comprehensive benchmarks that encompass various OOD perspectives to better understand and improve the robustness of neural retrieval models.
