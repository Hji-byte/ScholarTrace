# Literature Review

How do major federated learning optimization methods address client heterogeneity and non-IID data, and how do their assumptions differ?

## Summary
Federated learning (FL) optimization methods employ diverse mechanisms to address client heterogeneity and non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data [1, 3]. Approaches range from proximal regularization and variance reduction to synchronization-based aggregation and personalized modeling [1, 3, 8]. While these methods mitigate client drift and improve convergence under certain conditions, they rely on varying theoretical assumptions and face distinct limitations regarding system constraints, training instability, and computational overhead [2, 3, 6].

## Algorithmic Mechanisms for Addressing Heterogeneity
To mitigate the effects of data heterogeneity and client drift, researchers have developed several algorithmic modifications. Proximal and variance reduction methods, such as FedProx and SCAFFOLD, incorporate a proximal regularization term into local objectives to penalize deviations from the global model [1, 3] and employ control variates to offset drift in local updates, respectively [1, 3]. Normalization and local adaptation techniques address specific heterogeneity types; for instance, FedBN keeps local batch normalization parameters separate to address feature space heterogeneity [1, 6, 9], while FedNova employs a normalized averaging technique to correct objective inconsistencies caused by varying client update frequencies [1]. 

More recent approaches frame aggregation as a coordination problem. Kuramoto-FedAvg addresses gradient misalignment by framing weight aggregation as a synchronization problem inspired by the Kuramoto model, where the server dynamically weighs each client's update based on its phase alignment with the global gradient direction [1]. Personalized and asynchronous methods, such as APFL and PAFedFV, address heterogeneous data by learning personalized local models [3] and utilizing personalized model aggregation alongside asynchronous training modules to optimize client waiting time [8]. 

To handle model heterogeneity alongside statistical heterogeneity, Decentralized Learning with Model Heterogeneity (DL-MH) targets resource-constrained cross-device settings [2]. Knowledge distillation methods, including FedMD, Cronus, and FedDF, address model heterogeneity by transferring output parameters (logits) or utilizing public and unlabeled datasets, allowing clients to design unique model architectures [5]. Hierarchical and game-theoretic methods, such as LEAP, address non-IID data and limited communication by combining edge data distribution with a coalition formation game and gradient projection to dynamically adjust client-server correlations [4]. Other approaches mitigate non-IID problems through trust-based data sharing among coalition masters or by using the mean-shift algorithm to cluster clients based on data distribution for participant selection [4]. Finally, Elastic Federated Learning (EFL) tackles statistical heterogeneity and low participation rates by making informative parameters less volatile and utilizing incomplete local updates [3], while other methods leverage local steps, partial participation, Random Reshuffling, and gradient clipping to address non-convex optimization without extra restrictive assumptions [7].

## Theoretical Assumptions and System Constraints
The efficacy of federated optimization methods is heavily dependent on their underlying theoretical assumptions and system constraints. A fundamental assumption in standard FL is that all clients possess sufficient computational resources to train the same complex model architecture, an assumption that proves unrealistic in real-world scenarios with varying device capabilities and business needs [2]. Furthermore, early theoretical analyses of Local Training (LT) methods relied on restrictive data homogeneity assumptions that are often invalid in practical FL settings [7]. 

Algorithmic constraints also limit the applicability of certain methods. For example, FedProx requires careful hyperparameter tuning to balance convergence speed and stability [1]. Additionally, local correction techniques like FedNova and FedBN treat each client’s update independently, failing to directly address the root cause of client drift, which is the misalignment of gradient directions across clients [1]. Similarly, techniques such as FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedNova, and FedBN mitigate client drift through local constraints or static normalization schemes but lack mechanisms for dynamically coordinating the global alignment of client updates [1]. Standard FedAvg variants also fail to fully address system bias in non-IID settings, and existing workarounds, such as using a small shared global data subset or multiple models, often result in sub-optimal performance or increased communication costs [3].

## Convergence and Performance Under Non-IID Conditions
The convergence guarantees and empirical performance of FL algorithms vary significantly under different degrees of data heterogeneity. Standard FedAvg demonstrates a baseline ability to handle a certain amount of unbalanced and non-IID data but suffers from performance degradation or divergence when deployed over highly non-IID samples [2, 6]. While optimization methods such as FedProx, SCAFFOLD, and APFL achieve comparably fast convergence on IID data, they suffer considerable performance degradation in non-IID settings [3]. Specifically, as data becomes more non-IID, the convergence of FedProx degrades and can even diverge [3]. Similarly, although SCAFFOLD and APFL alleviate data heterogeneity, they exhibit instability during training [3]. 

In contrast, synchronization-based methods show improved theoretical and empirical performance. Kuramoto-FedAvg provides a tighter convergence bound and significantly accelerates convergence compared to standard FedAvg and FedProx under heterogeneous data distributions [1]. However, some heterogeneity-mitigating methods introduce practical overhead; for instance, FedBN addresses feature space heterogeneity but introduces additional computational and communication overhead [1, 6, 9].

## Limitations and Open Problems
Despite significant progress, several limitations and open problems remain in federated optimization for non-IID settings. Many existing methods rely on static normalization or local constraints, lacking the dynamic mechanisms necessary to coordinate the global alignment of client updates and resolve gradient misalignment [1]. The reliance on unrealistic assumptions regarding uniform computational resources across clients continues to hinder the scalability of standard FL frameworks [2]. Furthermore, addressing system bias often incurs sub-optimal performance or prohibitive communication costs [3]. Training instability under high degrees of non-IID data remains a critical issue for methods like SCAFFOLD and APFL [3], and the additional computational and communication overhead introduced by feature-alignment techniques like FedBN presents a barrier to resource-constrained deployments [6, 9]. Future work must focus on developing dynamic, globally coordinated aggregation strategies that operate efficiently under realistic system and statistical heterogeneity constraints.
