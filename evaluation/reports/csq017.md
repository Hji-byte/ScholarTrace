# Literature Review

Which workloads, metrics, and experimental methodologies are used to evaluate datacenter cluster schedulers?

## Summary
Evaluating datacenter cluster schedulers relies on a diverse array of real-world and synthetic workloads [1, 8], multidimensional performance metrics [5, 7], and experimental methodologies that combine simulation with physical testbeds [2, 3]. While researchers utilize community-shared traces and open-source simulation platforms [1], the field currently lacks a unified conceptual model for standardized evaluation [1]. Consequently, recent efforts have focused on proposing reference architectures and global competitions to facilitate reproducible and comparable scheduler assessments [1].

## Workload Characterization and Traces
Scheduler evaluations heavily utilize real-world workload traces collected from diverse environments [1, 2, 3, 4]. Common sources include engineering grid clusters (Askalon), private cloud IoT monitoring systems (Chronos), production deep learning traces (SenseTime), and Google cluster job traces [1, 2, 3, 4]. For machine learning cluster schedulers, evaluations specifically use job traces that capture diversity in job sizes, model types, arrival patterns, and specific compute requirements such as time per iteration and GPU demands [2, 4]. In the context of High-Performance Computing (HPC), evaluations employ a mix of real scientific workflows to test co-scheduling and resource allocation within dedicated clusters [7]. 

When physical traces are unavailable or specific behaviors need to be isolated, synthetic workloads are structured into distinct phases to simulate realistic dynamics [8]. These phases typically include a warm-up of small jobs, a burst of large long jobs, a steady phase, and a short-job tail [8].

## Performance Metrics and Evaluation Criteria
The effectiveness of cluster scheduling algorithms is assessed using a comprehensive mix of system-level and user-level metrics [1, 3, 5, 7, 8]. System-level metrics include node utilization, burst buffer utilization, overall system utilization, and cluster CPU utilization [1, 3, 5, 7, 8]. User-level metrics encompass average job wait time, average job completion time, job slowdown, and various time-based metrics such as total runtime, response time, waiting time, and turnaround time (TRT) distribution [1, 3, 5, 7, 8]. Specific reference architecture evaluations also measure targeted metrics like TRT, JMS, NJSL, JWT, and TC [1].

Beyond basic time and utilization metrics, advanced evaluations incorporate resource utilization (CPU, memory, PCIe transmission), fairness (fraction of unfairly scheduled jobs, worst-case slowdown), co-location goodness (interference of co-located jobs), and overall efficiency and responsiveness (makespan, utilization, average Job Completion Time) [2, 3, 6, 7]. To provide a holistic view of overall scheduling performance, researchers sometimes visualize these multidimensional metrics using Kiviat (radar) charts [8].

## Experimental Methodologies and Simulation Environments
Experimental methodologies combine event-driven simulations, trace-driven simulations that model datacenter topologies, and real-world experiments on physical testbeds [1, 2, 3, 6]. Physical testbeds used in recent studies include managed Kubernetes services, 34-node YARN/Spark clusters, 32-GPU clusters, and simulated heterogeneous CPU clusters [1, 2, 3, 6]. To evaluate scalability, simulators are often built using the exact same scheduler code as the physical clusters; the fidelity of these simulators is validated against physical testbed runs, demonstrating an average performance difference of around 5% [2].

To ensure statistical reliability, experiments are repeated multiple times with discarded warm-up iterations to mitigate the influence of underlying platforms and cold caches [1]. Proposed schedulers are rigorously compared against established baselines, such as YARN's default scheduling, static CPU quotas, and Slurm settings with backfilling [1, 6, 7]. Furthermore, HPC scheduling algorithms are frequently evaluated by integrating them directly into existing resource managers like Slurm and Mesos to test their practical viability [7].

## Standardization and Unified Frameworks
To address the fragmentation in evaluation methodologies, researchers leverage community-shared resources and propose unified frameworks [1]. The Grid Workloads Archive is utilized as a primary source of community-shared traces from real-world environments, providing standardized inputs for scheduler experiments [1]. Additionally, OpenDC serves as an open-source, community-driven simulation platform that has been extended to evaluate datacenter scheduling policies [1]. 

Recognizing the costly lack of unified models, proposed reference architectures aim to provide a community-wide underlying conceptual model to analyze, compare, and standardize the construction of datacenter schedulers [1]. Looking forward, community-wide experimental platforms and global competitions are proposed to further standardize evaluations, where scheduler submissions would run against specified workloads and resources in strictly reproducible experiments [1].

## Limitations and Open Problems
A primary limitation in current datacenter scheduler research is the lack of a standardized reference architecture or community-wide underlying conceptual model [1]. This absence makes it costly and difficult to systematically analyze and compare schedulers, leading to a landscape where many new scheduling policies appear annually but few are well-understood or adopted in practice [1]. While open-source platforms like OpenDC and repositories like the Grid Workloads Archive provide foundational standardization [1], the realization of fully unified, community-wide evaluation frameworks and global competitions remains an open problem and a long-term vision for the field [1].
